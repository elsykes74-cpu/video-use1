In the summer of 1968, Scotty Moore was forty-five years old and working as a recording engineer at a small studio in Nashville called Music City Recorders. He had been doing session work and studio maintenance for the better part of the decade. He was good at it. He kept regular hours. He knew the equipment. He had a reputation as someone who showed up on time and did the job without needing to be the most important person in the room.

Almost nobody outside of Nashville knew who he was.

This was not because Scotty Moore had done nothing of consequence. Fourteen years earlier, on the afternoon of July 5th, 1954, Scotty Moore had been in a small recording studio in Memphis, Tennessee, playing guitar alongside a nineteen-year-old truck driver who was in the middle of a session that was going nowhere, when something happened. The truck driver — whose name was Elvis Presley, who had paid four dollars to record a song for his mother's birthday a year earlier and had been invited back for a proper session — started playing a song in a way nobody had intended. He started jumping on it, Scotty Moore later said, just acting the fool. And Scotty Moore, without planning to, played a guitar lick behind him.

Sam Phillips — the studio owner, the man recording them — stuck his head out of the control room and told them to back up. He wanted to find out what that was. What it was was a record called That's All Right. It was released ten days later. It changed the music industry permanently.

Scotty Moore's guitar lick is the first sound on that record. Before Elvis sings a word, before the bass comes in, before any of what followed for the next quarter century, there is Scotty Moore's guitar. Eight notes. The ignition sound of rock and roll.

By the summer of 1968, Scotty Moore had not played with Elvis Presley in approximately ten years.

---

The break had not been sudden. The Army had taken Elvis in 1958, and during those two years Colonel Tom Parker had maintained Elvis's commercial presence through existing recordings and strategic rereleases. When Elvis came home in 1960, Parker had a new plan: movies. Film contracts were cleaner than touring. The money was predictable. The logistics were manageable. Parker could control everything.

The movie era required a different kind of musical support than the Sun Records sessions or the early touring years. It required session musicians — professionals who could read a chart, hit the mark, and leave. Scotty Moore and Bill Black, Elvis's original bass player, were not that kind of musician. They were the musicians who had built the thing in the first place, which is a different role and a less useful one once the building is done.

They were quietly set aside.

Scotty went back to session work and eventually to studio work. He watched Elvis make thirty-one films across the nineteen-sixties, films in which the songs got shallower and the plots got thinner and the man who had once made something permanent in a Memphis studio in 1954 receded behind an increasingly polished and decreasingly interesting commercial product.

Bill Black, Elvis's original bass player, died in October 1965 from a brain tumor. He was thirty-nine years old. He and Elvis had not spoken properly in years. There was no public acknowledgment from Graceland.

Scotty Moore read about it in the newspaper.

---

In early 1968, a television producer named Steve Binder was hired by NBC to produce Elvis Presley's Christmas special.

Binder was in his early thirties. He had directed the T.A.M.I. Show in 1964, a concert film featuring the Rolling Stones, James Brown, and others, and he had opinions about what live performance could do on film when it was allowed to be itself. He had opinions, more generally, about authenticity. He was not a man inclined to produce a Christmas special.

Colonel Parker had a specific vision for the broadcast. Elvis would stand in front of a set that looked like Christmas, and he would sing Christmas songs, and it would be watched by many people who liked Christmas, and the money would be satisfactory. Parker had been executing this kind of vision for a decade and it had never stopped working.

Binder told NBC that if that was what the special was going to be, they should find someone else to make it.

There were meetings. There were disagreements. There was a period during which it was not entirely clear whether the special would be made at all, or made in the form Parker wanted, or made in the form Binder wanted, or made in some form that satisfied neither of them. Parker was not a man who lost arguments. He had spent fifteen years not losing arguments with people who were more famous, more powerful, and more connected than Steve Binder.

In one of their meetings, held inside a cramped broom closet that Parker had cleared out to use as a makeshift office — with two uniformed William Morris agents standing guard outside to project an illusion of absolute authority — Parker tried to corner Binder. He demanded that Elvis wear a tuxedo and sing Christmas carols. Elvis was in the room, his head down and his eyes lowered, playing the part of the obedient client. "Yes, sir," he said. But the second they stepped out of the closet and away from Parker's gaze, Elvis jabbed Binder in the ribs. "Fuck him," he whispered. "We're not going to worry about that."

Still, Binder had to navigate NBC's financial commitment, and the schedule had to be met, and at some point in this process Binder won enough of the argument to be allowed to make a different kind of show.

He called Scotty Moore.

He also called D.J. Fontana, Elvis's original drummer, who had been with him since 1955 and had not played with him in years.

He did not call any of the musicians who had worked Elvis's Las Vegas engagements or his studio sessions through the sixties. He called the people who had been there at the beginning.

---

During the rehearsals at their offices on Sunset Boulevard, Elvis was locked in his own myth, terrified of the public. Steve Binder decided to test that myth. He asked Elvis what he thought would happen if they walked out onto the street. Elvis turned the question back on him. "Nothing," Binder said. "You'll walk out there, and nobody will pay any attention to you. Times have changed."

Elvis was skeptical, but he wanted to see. At five o'clock in the evening, during peak traffic, they went downstairs alone, leaving the entourage to watch from the third-story window. They stood on the pavement of the Sunset Strip outside a topless bar. For ten minutes, they loitered. Cars drove by, but no horns honked. Pedestrians walked past. A couple of seedy-looking hippies, deeply engaged in their own conversation, literally bumped into Elvis Presley and kept walking, never once lifting their heads.

It was a quiet, almost embarrassing revelation. Elvis tried to wave at cars, trying to get someone, anyone, to notice him, but the world had moved on while he was in Hollywood. When they went back upstairs, Binder noticed a profound change in Elvis's demeanor. He was loose, he was laughing, and he finally trusted Binder. The fear of being a prisoner to his own celebrity was gone; what remained was the stark realization that he had to fight to be relevant again.

But as the taping in Burbank approached, a different kind of fear set in. Elvis Presley had not performed live in front of an audience in seven years.

He knew the special was happening. He knew it was going to be on television. He knew that people would be watching who remembered what he used to be, and he knew that the distance between what he used to be and what he had been doing for a decade was something that would be visible to all of them.

Binder later said that in the weeks before the taping, Elvis was more frightened than he had ever seen a performer be. Not stage fright in the ordinary sense — not the nerves that come before any performance — but a deeper fear. The fear of someone who has been away from a thing for long enough that they are no longer certain they still know how to do it.

He had spent ten years not being allowed to be himself. He had spent ten years playing characters in films and singing songs that had been chosen for him and performing in a context that had been designed to produce income rather than art. He had been polished and managed and photographed and sold, and somewhere in that process the thing that had made the polishing worth doing had gotten harder to find.

On the evening of June 27th, 1968, in NBC's Burbank studios, they set up a small stage — more like a platform — and put folding chairs in a circle around it. There were a few microphones, a few amplifiers. No elaborate production. No choreography.

Colonel Parker had promised to handle ticket distribution, reassuring the producers that he would pack the studio with loyal, screaming fans from across the country. But when the day of the taping arrived, the line outside the studio was completely empty. Parker had never distributed the tickets. In a panic, Binder and executive producer Bob Finkel sent their crew across the street to Bob's Big Boy, recruiting bewildered civilians who were in the middle of eating hamburgers. They weren't devotees; they were just regular people who had been pulled off their stools to fill an empty room, leaving little space between the performer and the people watching.

Scotty Moore came in with his guitar. D.J. Fontana came in with his sticks. Elvis came in, sat down in one of the chairs, and for a moment the three of them were just there together — the same three people who had sat in a studio in Memphis in 1954 and made something that had changed everything, fourteen years later and several thousand miles away, in a room that looked nothing like that room.

They started playing.

What happened next was not planned and has never been fully explained. Elvis began to relax. Not slowly, not with visible effort — he just, at some point in the session, stopped being afraid. He joked. He made mistakes and laughed at them.

At one point, he traded instruments with Scotty, taking Scotty's cherry-red 1963 Gibson Super 400. Because Scotty had assumed they would be sitting down the entire evening, he hadn't brought a guitar strap. When Elvis stood up to perform "Blue Suede Shoes," he found himself struggling to hold the heavy electric guitar, forced to prop it awkwardly on his knee with the help of his chair.

"We don't have a strap?" Elvis asked Scotty.

"No," Scotty said.

"I gotta keep my leg here all night?"

"Yep," Scotty replied, watching the expensive body of his guitar wobble in Elvis's hands. "Watch it."

But Elvis played anyway, his rhythm tough and exciting, his fingers finding the old blues chords. He played songs that had not been in any of his films or any of his Las Vegas sets, songs from the Sun Records period, songs from the early touring years, songs that nobody had asked him to play in a decade. He played them the way he had played them the first time, loose and alive.

Scotty Moore said afterward that it was like watching someone remember something they had been told to forget.

The session went long. Nobody stopped it. They played for hours. The cameras caught most of it.

That footage became the emotional center of the special.

---

The '68 Comeback Special broadcast on NBC on December 3rd, 1968.

It was the highest-rated television program of the season.

The reviews were unlike anything Elvis Presley had received in fifteen years. Not polite reviews, not the qualified praise of critics who thought he should have done better — genuine reviews, from people who had watched something happen in that broadcast that they had not expected to see.

The closing number was not a Christmas song. It was a direct response to a year that was tearing the country apart. Martin Luther King Jr. had been assassinated in Memphis in April, and only three weeks before the taping, on the night of June 5th, Robert F. Kennedy had been shot in Los Angeles. The country was bleeding, and Binder wanted Elvis to say something that mattered — to offer an answer to the chaos. He commissioned a new song from Earl Brown called If I Can Dream. When Elvis sang it, standing on a stage in a white suit in front of his name in towering red lights, it went out to thirty million viewers, and for approximately four minutes the distance between who Elvis Presley had been in 1956 and who he was in 1968 closed completely.

Parker watched the broadcast from a hotel room in Las Vegas. He had lost the argument about the Christmas songs. He had lost the argument about the format. He had lost the argument about bringing in Scotty Moore and D.J. Fontana. The show had been made the way Binder wanted to make it, and it had been watched by thirty million people and the reviews were the best Elvis had received since the Ed Sullivan appearances in 1956.

Parker said that he had known all along it would work out.

---

In the months after the special, there were meetings about what came next. The obvious answer, to almost everyone involved except Parker, was that what came next was touring — real touring, internationally, the kind of concerts that a man who could hold thirty million television viewers could fill anywhere on earth.

Parker deflected those conversations the way he always deflected those conversations. Scheduling. Logistics. The right time hadn't come. There were domestic commitments to consider first.

What Parker arranged instead was Las Vegas. The International Hotel, beginning the summer of 1969.

Elvis went.

The first Vegas engagement was a success by any financial measure. The second was. The third was. By the early nineteen-seventies, Elvis was performing over a hundred shows a year in Las Vegas, in a showroom that held two thousand people, and the world that had just rediscovered what he was capable of was watching him do it from seats that could only be reached by flying to Nevada.

Scotty Moore was not invited back. He had done his part. The informal session in the Burbank studio had produced some of the most important footage in the special, and Scotty Moore's guitar was all over it, and that was done now.

He went back to Nashville. He continued his studio work. He gave occasional interviews about the early days and answered, many times, the same questions about what it was like in the Sun Records studio in 1954, and he answered them with the patience of someone who understood that the story was bigger than he was.

---

Elvis Presley died on August 16th, 1977. He was forty-two years old.

Scotty Moore heard about it the way most people heard about it — from the news. He was in Nashville. He had not spoken to Elvis in years.

He said afterward that it felt like losing a brother. Not a friend, not a colleague — a brother. The way you can not speak to someone for years and still feel the specific weight of their absence.

He said something else, in an interview, that was less quoted but perhaps more true. He said that in that Burbank studio in 1968, he had watched Elvis find the thing again. The thing that had been there in Memphis in 1954. He had watched it come back. And he had thought, sitting there with his guitar in the circle of chairs, that it was going to be all right from then on.

He was wrong about that. But he was right about what he saw.

---

There is a guitar in the Rock and Roll Hall of Fame in Cleveland, Ohio. It is a Bigsby-equipped Gibson, pale and worn, with pick marks on the body and the specific worn quality of an instrument that has been played seriously for a long time.

It belongs to Scotty Moore. It is the guitar he played in that NBC studio in Burbank on June 27th, 1968. It is the guitar you can hear on the informal sit-down footage that became the center of the comeback special.

The Hall of Fame opened in 1995. Elvis was inducted in 1986, the first year it was possible to be inducted — the eligibility rule requires twenty-five years to have passed since your first recording, and Elvis's first recording was in 1954, and 1954 plus twenty-five is 1979, and the Hall opened six years after that.

His induction plaque says he was the most important figure in the history of popular music. It says he changed the culture. It says he was the King of Rock and Roll.

It does not say anything about a studio in Burbank in 1968, or a circle of folding chairs, or a guitarist who drove in from Nashville and sat down across from someone he had not played with in ten years, and played the first song, and watched the thing come back.

It doesn't need to. The footage exists. You can watch it. You can see exactly when it happens — the precise moment, in that small informal session, when Elvis Presley stops being afraid.

It is not a dramatic moment. It is quiet. He laughs at something, and then he starts playing, and something in his shoulders changes, and that is it. That is the whole story.
