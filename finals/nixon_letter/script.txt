By the winter of 1970, Elvis Presley had been the most famous person on the planet for fourteen years. He had sold more records than any solo artist in history. He had made thirty-one movies. He had performed for millions of people. He had spent so much of his life being managed, scheduled, and controlled that he had stopped asking, in any serious way, what he himself might want to do next.

What he had started doing instead was collecting things.

He collected guns. He collected karate uniforms. He collected police badges — from sheriff's departments in Memphis, Nashville, Denver, Palm Springs, from any officer who would trade a photograph for a credential. By 1970 he had several dozen of them, mounted in display cases at Graceland and at his house in Bel Air. The collection had a logic to it that was different from the logic of fame. Fame was given to you. A badge meant someone had officially decided you were useful.

He had been given exactly that kind of official usefulness once before, and he had not forgotten it. In 1958, at the age of twenty-three, at the height of his early success, Elvis Presley had been inducted into the United States Army. He had been Private Presley for two years — not an entertainer, not a product, just a soldier — and he had found in that role a clarity that the music business had never given him. People told him what to do and why, and he did it, and at the end of the day the purpose was clear.

He had come home from the Army in 1960 and walked back into the career that Colonel Tom Parker had maintained in his absence, and the career was fine, and the money was enormous, and it did not feel anything like being useful.

In 1968, something had briefly changed. A television producer named Steve Binder had fought with Parker and won a single argument, and the result was a live performance broadcast on NBC in December that reminded the world — and Elvis — of what he actually was. The special was the highest-rated show of the season. The reviews were the best of Elvis's career.

Parker had responded to this by booking Elvis into the International Hotel in Las Vegas.

The money was extraordinary. The crowds were large. The commissions were satisfying. Elvis performed for a month, then went home to Graceland, then came back and performed for another month. He wore jumpsuits that got more elaborate each time. He played songs people recognized. He was, by any external measurement, thriving.

His personal physician, a Memphis doctor named George Nichopoulos, was writing prescriptions for him at a volume that had been quietly escalating since the mid-nineteen-sixties. The sedatives helped him sleep. The stimulants helped him wake up. The painkillers handled a rotating series of complaints. The prescriptions moved through his household at a rate that, as a later investigation would find, amounted to thousands of doses a year.

His wife Priscilla had married a man she'd known since she was fourteen and he was twenty-four, and the man she had married was no longer quite the man she was living with.

On the evening of December 20th, 1970, they had an argument. The specifics of the argument are not on the record. What is on the record is what Elvis did next. He left. He drove himself to the airport — not the private terminal, the regular one — and he bought a ticket at the counter with cash for a commercial flight to Washington D.C.

He was not supposed to be going to Washington D.C. He had no appointment there, no meeting, no plan. He was going because he needed to go somewhere and the ticket was available.

Somewhere over the Midwest, he took out a legal pad and began to write.

---

He kept writing on the connection to Dallas. He kept writing on the return flight to Washington. By the time he landed at Dulles at six in the morning, he had filled several pages. He folded them into an envelope, addressed it to the President of the United States, and took a cab to the White House.

He arrived at the Northwest Gate at approximately six thirty in the morning.

The security officer on duty looked at the man in the purple velvet suit and the floor-length cape and the oversized sunglasses. He took the envelope. He said he would pass it along. Elvis said thank you and waited.

The envelope was taken inside.

---

Egil Krogh was thirty-one years old and had been working in the Nixon White House for two years. His title was deputy counsel to the President. His actual job was to handle things that didn't fit anywhere else — the unusual requests, the unexpected arrivals, the pieces of paper that didn't have a department to land in. He worked directly under John Ehrlichman and took his work seriously with the focus of a man who had not yet learned what focus in the wrong direction could cost.

He opened the envelope at his desk and read the letter.

It was not a fan letter. It was not a petition or a complaint or a request for a photograph. It was a job application, written in careful ballpoint pen on yellow legal pad paper across several pages.

Elvis Presley wrote that he had been studying America's drug crisis and found it alarming. He wrote that the counterculture had been used to turn young Americans against their country, against their values, against the institutions that held the nation together. He named the Beatles specifically as an example of foreign musical influence weaponized against the republic. He wrote that the government was losing the war for young people's loyalty not because it lacked resources but because it lacked access — and that he had access.

People trusted him inside the counterculture in ways they would never trust a man in a government suit. He could go to places and talk to people that no federal agent could reach. He was offering his services in whatever capacity the President found appropriate.

What he wanted in return was the badge and credentials of a Federal Agent at Large with the Bureau of Narcotics and Dangerous Drugs.

Not a salary. Not a title. Not an office or a security clearance or any formal government position. Just the badge.

Krogh read the letter twice, made some notes, and called his secretary in.

He had a memo ready for H.R. Haldeman — the President's chief of staff — within two hours.

---

The memo was four paragraphs. It had to accomplish several things at once: establish who Elvis Presley was, which was not something Krogh could assume Nixon knew in any meaningful way; explain why the meeting was worth the President's time; and frame the badge request in language that made it manageable rather than alarming.

Krogh was good at this. He explained, in the first paragraph, that Elvis Presley was the most commercially successful recording artist in American history — fifty-six gold records, two hundred million records sold, global recognition that no political figure could manufacture. He noted the Army service. He noted that Elvis had not aligned himself with the anti-war movement and had publicly opposed several of its cultural figures. He noted that two of the President's daughters were fans.

The second paragraph described the potential benefit of a brief Oval Office meeting. The optics were straightforward: the President meeting with a patriotic American entertainer was a positive image in a news cycle that was producing very few of those.

The third paragraph addressed the counterintelligence offer with diplomatic understatement. The proposal was creative, Krogh wrote. Its practical implementation would require further discussion. It could be graciously acknowledged without committing the Bureau to any specific course of action.

The fourth paragraph recommended that the President agree to a short meeting.

Haldeman approved it within the hour. Nixon was briefed. A noon appointment was found in the schedule.

---

There are photographs of the meeting. Every meeting in the Oval Office was documented by the official White House photographer. These were classified as routine and declassified in 1972.

In the photographs, Nixon stands next to Elvis. Both are smiling. Nixon's arm is around Elvis's shoulder. The President is in a dark suit, managing his expression with the careful precision of a man who had been managing his expression in photographs for twenty-five years. Elvis is in the purple velvet and the gold belt, the sunglasses pushed up onto his forehead. He is broader than Nixon, taller by several inches, and he looks the way he always looked in photographs — as though the camera was invented specifically for him to stand in front of.

Nixon spoke about his daughters. He mentioned that they were fans. He spoke about the drug problem and what it was doing to American values. He spoke about the counterculture and what it was costing the country. He asked Elvis how he stayed in shape.

Elvis was patient. He nodded. He said the right things. He waited for the right moment.

Then he made his ask.

He explained, carefully and seriously, that he had the contacts and the access and the goodwill inside communities that the Bureau of Narcotics and Dangerous Drugs could not reach through conventional channels. Young Americans who had been told all their lives that government was the enemy still listened to Elvis Presley. He could go places. He could get information. He could be useful in ways that did not appear on any official personnel roster.

What he wanted, in exchange, was the badge.

The Bureau of Narcotics and Dangerous Drugs agent present in the room began to explain why this was procedurally complicated. A Federal Agent at Large credential carried legal weight and certain formal obligations and implied a level of accountability that —

Nixon turned from the agent to Elvis, and then back to the agent again.

The agent excused himself to make a telephone call.

Twenty minutes later, Elvis Presley walked out of the Oval Office with a Bureau of Narcotics and Dangerous Drugs Federal Agent at Large badge in his pocket. He also left with presidential cufflinks for five members of his entourage, a signed photograph for his father Vernon, and a personal note for Priscilla — written, presumably, in the Oval Office, within an hour of leaving the house after an argument with her.

At twelve forty-seven in the afternoon, he walked back out through the Northwest Gate — past the spot where he had stood in the dark six hours earlier — and got into a waiting car.

---

What nobody said in the Oval Office that afternoon was what Elvis Presley had been carrying in his luggage during the overnight commercial flights that had brought him to Washington.

The toiletries bag in his suitcase contained prescription medications not prescribed to him and obtained through a process that, had anyone at the Bureau looked closely, would have been difficult to explain. The prescriptions that moved through Elvis Presley's household in 1970 were the product of a medical relationship that would later become the subject of a formal Tennessee Board of Medical Examiners investigation. Doctor Nichopoulos — whom Elvis called Doctor Nick — had been prescribing at levels that, by any standard measure, had long since left the realm of clinical medicine.

Elvis did not think of himself as addicted. He thought of himself as medicated. These were prescriptions, written by a licensed physician, for a patient who needed them. He was not a drug user. He was going to help the government fight drug users. He had the badge to prove it.

The badge went into a display case at Graceland, in the trophy room, next to the honorary commission from the Memphis Police Department and the certificate from the Denver sheriff's office.

The Bureau of Narcotics and Dangerous Drugs filed no record of any undercover operation conducted by Federal Agent at Large Elvis Aaron Presley.

No mission. No contact. No report.

Just a badge number, assigned and never used.

---

Two weeks after the meeting, a thank-you letter arrived at the White House from Memphis, Tennessee. It was handwritten on personalized stationery, the name embossed at the top, the handwriting deliberate and unhurried. It thanked the President for his time and restated the writer's continuing commitment to the cause they had discussed. It mentioned the badge, and what it meant to him.

It was signed, at the bottom:

Respectfully, Elvis Presley.

---

Egil Krogh — the thirty-one-year-old who had written the four paragraphs that opened the Oval Office door — continued to rise in the Nixon administration for the next eighteen months. In the summer of 1971, he was assigned to lead a covert unit whose purpose was to stop unauthorized information from leaving the White House. The unit acquired an informal name. They were called the Plumbers — because they were there to fix leaks.

In September of 1971, the unit broke into the Beverly Hills office of a psychiatrist named Lewis Fielding. They were looking for files on Daniel Ellsberg, a former Pentagon analyst who had given classified documents about the Vietnam War to the New York Times. The documents had become known as the Pentagon Papers. The break-in was illegal. They did not find what they were looking for.

Krogh pleaded guilty in 1973 to violating Daniel Ellsberg's civil rights. He was sentenced to six months in federal prison. He served four and a half.

He was disbarred. He was later reinstated. He spent decades writing and speaking about government ethics and what happens when loyalty becomes a substitute for judgment. He wrote a memoir. He gave lectures. He said, many times, in different words, that he had asked the wrong question at the crucial moment. He had asked what the President would want, instead of asking what was right.

He never wrote publicly about the morning of December 21st, 1970. About the envelope from the Northwest Gate. About the four paragraphs he had dictated to his secretary while the man in the purple velvet suit waited outside.

He did not need to. The letter is in the National Archives. The memo is in the National Archives. The thank-you note is in the National Archives. Anyone who wants to read them can.

The photograph is in the Smithsonian Institution.

The badge is in a glass case at Graceland, in the trophy room, where it has been since January 1971.

---

Elvis Presley died on August 16th, 1977. He was forty-two years old. The cause of death was cardiac arrhythmia, related to the interaction of multiple prescription medications taken in combination and at elevated doses. Doctor Nichopoulos, his personal physician, was later found to have prescribed Elvis over ten thousand doses of various drugs in the two and a half years before his death — an average of more than ten doses a day.

The Bureau of Narcotics and Dangerous Drugs had become the Drug Enforcement Administration in 1973. The DEA filed no record of any work performed by Elvis Aaron Presley on its behalf during the seven years between the badge request and his death.

In a filing cabinet in Washington D.C., in a row of grey metal shelves in a federally maintained archive, there is a manila folder that is slightly more worn than the ones around it. It has been requested by researchers more than most of its neighbors. The folder holds three pieces of paper: the original letter, the Krogh memo, and the thank-you note.

On the folder's tab, there is a typed reference label.

Presley, Elvis A. — Correspondence, December 1970 — January 1971.

Below the typed label, in pencil, in handwriting that does not belong to any official on record, someone added two words at some point during the decades the folder has been sitting there.

Badge request.

That is the full official record of the day the most famous man in America woke up in the middle of the night, flew across the country on a series of commercial flights, wrote a letter by hand on a yellow legal pad, walked up to the gate of the White House before sunrise, and asked to be made a federal agent.

It fit in a folder.

It always does.
