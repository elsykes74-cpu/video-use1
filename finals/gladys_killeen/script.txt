There is a photograph taken at Fort Chaffee, Arkansas, in March of 1958. Elvis Presley is sitting in a barber's chair. His hair is falling to the floor around him in dark clumps. He is smiling for the camera in the practiced way of a man who has been smiling for cameras since he was nineteen. But if you look at the photograph long enough, past the smile, you can see something in his eyes that the smile does not reach.

Standing off to the side of the frame, blurred, not quite in the shot, is a small, heavy-set woman in a dark dress. She is watching the barber work. She has traveled from Memphis to Fort Chaffee to watch her son have his hair cut by the United States Army. She has been watching over him for as long as there has been a him to watch over, and she is not going to stop now simply because the Army says he belongs to them.

Her name was Gladys Love Presley. She was forty-six years old. She had been unwell for some time. The doctors had a theory about what was wrong with her, and the theory would prove correct, and she would be dead in five months.

Neither of them knew that yet. She stood in the frame of the photograph and watched the barber, and Elvis smiled, and the Army got what it came for.

---

Gladys Smith was born in 1912 in Pontotoc County, Mississippi, the sixth of eight children, in circumstances that would have been described at the time as modest and that were, in practice, something much harder than modest. Her father was a sharecropper. The family moved frequently, following work. She met Vernon Presley when she was seventeen and married him when she was twenty-one, which was not young for a woman in Tupelo, Mississippi in 1933.

Their son was born on January 8, 1935. He was a twin — his brother Jesse Garon was stillborn. The surviving child was named Elvis Aaron.

What happened between Gladys Presley and her surviving son is difficult to describe in language that doesn't make it sound either ordinary or morbid, because it was neither. It was specific. She had held one twin and buried him and raised the other, and the one she raised understood from a very early age that he had been given something the other had not, and that the woman looking after him understood this more acutely than anyone else alive.

She called him by names — E, her baby, her own — that she did not use for anyone else. He called her Satnin, a nonsense word between them, a word that belonged to no one but the two of them. He bought her things when he had nothing to buy them with and continued buying her things when he had more money than either of them knew what to do with. He bought her a pink Cadillac and a house on Audubon Drive and then Graceland, a fourteen-room mansion on a hill outside Memphis, and when he moved into Graceland he moved his parents in with him.

People who knew Gladys Presley described her as sharp, funny, and worried. She worried in the specific way of someone who has learned that what you love can be taken without warning. She worried about Elvis's driving, about the crowds at concerts, about the girls who followed the car. She worried about the things he ate. She worried, in the later fifties, about what the money was doing to the people around him.

She was not wrong to worry. But she could not have worried about the specific thing that was coming in 1958, because no amount of worry could have found it in time.

---

The draft notice came in December 1957. Elvis Presley was twenty-two years old and had been the most famous person in America for two years. His manager — Colonel Tom Parker, a man who had made himself indispensable precisely because he understood money better than any artist ever bothered to — had quietly arranged a six-month deferment on the grounds of an ongoing film commitment. The deferment was granted. The film was completed. And on March 24th, 1958, Elvis Presley reported to the Memphis draft board and was inducted into the United States Army as a private.

He could have applied for Special Services, the division that handled entertainment — Bob Hope's Christmas shows, morale broadcasts, the touring units. Colonel Parker could have arranged it. The Army would have been glad to have him. The publicity alone would have been worth more than a dozen ordinary soldiers.

Parker advised against it. A man who served like every other man would come home to a public that had kept faith with him. A man who entertained in uniform might come home to a public that felt they had been sold something.

Elvis took the advice. He went to Fort Chaffee, Arkansas, for processing. Then to Fort Hood, Texas, for basic training.

And then something happened that was unusual for a soldier but was, for Elvis Presley, simply the way his life worked. He requested permission to live off-base, in Killeen, the town outside Fort Hood. The Army allowed it. He rented a house on East Oak Hill Drive. He brought in Vernon, his father. He brought in his grandmother Minnie Mae, who was in her mid-seventies and had never lived anywhere but Mississippi and Tennessee. And he brought in Gladys.

The house in Killeen was not Graceland. It was a small frame house in a Texas town whose economy existed entirely to support the base. It had a yard and a kitchen and rooms that were adequate and nothing more. Gladys arrived in April. She set up the household the way she always set up households — took charge of the kitchen, made it familiar, tried to make the strange feel ordinary.

She was not well. People who saw her in Killeen that spring described her as pale and exhausted and not herself. She had gained weight over the years at Graceland, and in Killeen she began to lose it, which is the kind of weight loss that does not feel like good news. She was drinking more than she had been — beer, mostly, in larger quantities than she acknowledged to anyone. She told people she was tired. She told Elvis she was fine.

He watched her the way she had always watched him. He noticed what she was telling him and he noticed what she was not.

---

The summer of 1958 in Killeen, Texas was the summer of waiting.

Elvis waited for weekends, when he could come home from the base. He waited for meals and phone calls and the ordinary relief of being inside four walls that were as close to his own walls as could be arranged. The Army did not give him Graceland. It gave him this house on East Oak Hill Drive and a schedule and a rank — he had been promoted to Private First Class in May — and the general understanding that what the Army gave you was better than what you'd come from, which was not a premise Elvis Presley needed to examine too carefully.

Gladys waited for him at the gate.

This is documented by people who lived near the base, by the family, by members of Elvis's entourage who visited that summer. She would drive to Fort Hood and park outside the gate and wait for the times she was allowed to see him. She did not wait with the patience of someone who has made peace with waiting. She waited with the urgency of someone who has a reason to be there that she cannot fully explain.

She had always had what her family called feelings. She had dreams that told her things. In Tupelo when Elvis was very small, she dreamed the house was going to flood and moved the family to higher ground before the creek rose. The people who described this later used the word premonition, which is a word that means different things depending on whether you believe in it or not. What is not disputed is that Gladys Presley had a sensory relationship with the world around her that was not strictly rational and that was, in her experience, worth listening to.

She did not tell Elvis, that summer in Killeen, what she was feeling. She told him she was tired. She told him the heat was getting to her. She told him the house was fine and the food was fine and he shouldn't worry.

Vernon Presley later said that she told him the truth. That she told him she did not think she was going to get better. That she told him she was going to die before Elvis came home.

Vernon said he told her she was wrong. He said he believed it.

---

In August, permission came for the household to return to Memphis. Gladys was going to see a doctor. The doctor in Memphis could look at her and they would find out what was wrong and they would fix it, because that is what doctors did.

She flew to Memphis on August 8th. Elvis was not on the flight. He was at Fort Hood. He called her from a phone on base, the way he had been calling her all summer, and she told him she was going to be fine and he told her he would see her soon.

She was admitted to Methodist Hospital in Memphis on August 9th, 1958. The diagnosis was hepatitis, though the picture was complicated by what the doctors were too careful to say plainly: that her liver had been damaged by years of alcohol use, that her heart was under strain, that her body was running out of reserves.

Elvis was granted emergency leave. He flew to Memphis. He was at the hospital. He sat with her. People who were there describe him as not knowing what to do with himself — a man who was used to managing everything around him, who could call anyone and have things fixed, sitting in a hospital corridor and understanding that this was not a thing that could be fixed.

He went home to Graceland on the night of August 13th. He was not at the hospital when she died.

---

Gladys Love Presley died at Methodist Hospital in Memphis, Tennessee, at approximately three fifteen in the morning on August 14th, 1958. She was forty-six years old.

The call came to Graceland in the middle of the night. Elvis was asleep. He was told, and he drove to the hospital. He arrived and he went in and he saw her, and what happened then has been described by everyone who was there as something they did not know how to describe. He was not a composed man. He did not hold himself together for the benefit of the people watching. He wept in a way that people who witnessed it had not seen a man weep in public, and he talked to her, using the names between them — Satnin, baby, mama — and he did not try to stop.

He had been twenty miles away when she died. He had been at Graceland, the house he had bought for her, in the room he had wanted her to sleep in forever. He had gone home for one night.

This was the thing he could not forgive. Not that she had died. Not that he had been in the Army and she had been in Killeen and summer in Texas is a brutal thing for a woman who is already ill. He could accommodate those things as facts. He could not accommodate the thing that was simpler and more specific: that on the night she died, he had been there, and he had gone home.

He talked about it for the rest of his life. Not often, and not to many people. But he talked about it. He said that she had always been there when it mattered. He said that when it mattered, he had not been.

---

The funeral was on August 15th. It was, by necessity, a public event, because there was no private version of Elvis Presley's life available in August 1958. The press was outside. The crowds were outside. Colonel Parker was involved in the logistics of managing what was happening in a way that could be photographed and published.

Inside the house, Elvis was inconsolable in the literal sense of the word — there was nothing that could be said to him that would console him, because the thing he had lost was not the kind of thing that could be replaced with words.

He said at the graveside things that people who were there remembered for the rest of their lives. He said that she was all he had. He said that she was the only real thing he had ever had. He said that everything else — the records, the money, the cars, Graceland itself — all of it had been for her, and without her it didn't mean anything, and he didn't know what he was going to do now.

He was twenty-three years old. The Army gave him seven days.

After seven days, he reported back to Fort Hood. Three weeks later, his unit shipped out to Germany. He spent the next eighteen months in Friedberg, in a country where almost nobody spoke his language, with a father who came to live with him there and who had lost as much as he had and did not have the same capacity to carry it.

Parker arranged for recordings he had already made to be released on a schedule. The records came out. The fans continued. The money continued. Parker managed it all with the efficiency of a man who had built a system that could survive the temporary removal of its central component.

Elvis performed none of it. He was a soldier in Germany. He ate in the mess hall and did his duties and wrote letters and went home to the house on Goethestrasse where Vernon and the household were, and he did not talk about his mother to most people, and the people who were close to him said he talked about her constantly.

---

He came home from the Army in March 1960. He was twenty-five. He stepped off the plane at McGuire Air Force Base in New Jersey and there were photographers, and Colonel Parker was there, and there were record label people and studio people and the machinery of a career waiting for him. He smiled. He wore his dress uniform. He gave the statement that had been prepared.

He went back to Graceland, and Gladys was not there.

The house was the same. His grandmother was there. His father was there. The rooms were the same rooms she had been in. The kitchen was the kitchen she had made familiar. The pink Cadillac was in the driveway.

He slept in the bedroom he had always slept in, in the house she had lived in for less than two years, and he went back to making records, and he went back to making movies, and Colonel Parker arranged everything, and the money was extraordinary, and for the next seventeen years he was the most famous man in the world.

There is no clean ending to the story of Gladys Presley and her son. There is no resolution. The story of what she was to him does not conclude so much as it continues, in everything that came after, in every account of Elvis Presley that observes, as nearly all of them eventually do, that something changed in him after August 1958. That the man who came back from Germany was not quite the same as the one who had left. That there was a quality in him — not sadness exactly, something more specific, something more like an awareness that the thing you are most afraid of losing can simply stop being there one morning while you are twenty miles away sleeping — that was not there before.

He had been afraid of many things. He had been afraid of the Army and afraid of what the movies would do to his music and afraid, as the sixties wore on, that he was becoming something other than what he had been. In 1968 a television producer named Steve Binder would get close enough to him to see the fear directly and build a television special around the project of making it stop.

But the fear that was at the bottom of all the other fears — the one that was there before any of the others — had its origin in a house in Killeen, Texas, in the summer of 1958, when a woman waited at the gate of a military base and told her son she was fine.

She was not fine.

He had known it. He had watched her the way she had always watched him, and he had seen what she was not saying, and he had believed what she told him instead of what he knew.

That is the part he could not forgive.

---

Gladys Love Presley is buried in Forest Hill Cemetery in Memphis, Tennessee, not far from the house on Lamar Avenue where she and Vernon lived when Elvis was beginning to be famous. When Elvis died in 1977, he was buried at Forest Hill as well — the two of them in adjacent plots, under matching stones.

He was later moved to the Meditation Garden at Graceland, the house he had bought for her. She was moved with him.

They are there now, the two of them, in the garden of the house she lived in for less than two years. The tour buses go past every day. The tourists file through. The stones have flowers on them, left by people who feel something they cannot explain when they stand there.

Most of them are there to see Elvis. Most of them are not sure, when they look at the stone next to his, whether they know who Gladys Presley was.

She was his first reason for everything. She was the person he was trying to give things to, from the first dollar he ever earned to the house on the hill. She was the person whose opinion was the one that mattered, in the specific way that only one person's opinion ever really matters, and she was the person who was not there when he needed to hear it most.

He said once, in an interview in the sixties, that he had never gotten over losing her. He said it the way you say a thing that you know is true and that no one around you fully understands.

He was right that they didn't. He was right that they wouldn't.

The Cadillac is still pink. The stones are still there. The story still ends at three fifteen in the morning, with a phone ringing in a house on the hill, and a man twenty miles away who should have stayed.
